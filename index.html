What GPA means

GPA stands for grade point average. Each letter grade is worth a set number of points, usually 4.0 for an A down to 0 for an F. Multiply each class's points by its credits to get quality points, then divide the total by the credits that count. The result is your GPA.

How to use the GPA calculator

  1. Pick College or High school. In high school mode, choose each class's level so Honors, AP and IB bonus points count.
  2. Enter each class's grade and credits. Add a term for every semester or quarter, and add your GPA so far if you want a cumulative GPA.
  3. Click Calculate GPA. You'll get your GPA with the math behind it, the class that would raise it most, a what-if tool and a planner for your target.

To send your result to a parent or counselor, use "Copy share link". The grades travel inside the link, not through our server.

Example: an A and a B

An A in a 3-credit class is 4.0 × 3 = 12 quality points. A B in a 4-credit class is 3.0 × 4 = 12. That's 24 quality points over 7 credits, and 24 ÷ 7 = 3.43.

The answer isn't 3.5. The B carries more credits, so it pulls the GPA down more than the A lifts it.

How to calculate your GPA by hand

To calculate a GPA, turn each letter grade into grade points, multiply by the class's credits, and divide the total by the credits that count.

  1. Convert each grade to grade points. On the common 4.0 scale an A is 4.0, a B is 3.0, a C is 2.0, a D is 1.0 and an F is 0.
  2. Multiply each class's grade points by its credits. That gives the class's quality points.
  3. Add up the quality points for all your classes.
  4. Add up the credits of the classes that count toward GPA.
  5. Divide the quality points by the credits. The result is your GPA.

With an A in a 3-credit class and a B in a 4-credit class, that's 12 + 12 = 24 quality points over 7 credits, so 24 ÷ 7 = 3.43.

Semester vs. cumulative GPA

Your semester GPA covers one term. Your cumulative GPA covers every graded class you've taken, so each new semester moves it less than the one before. Add a term for each semester above, and the calculator shows both: a GPA for each term and one for all of them together.

How the target GPA planner works

Set a target and the credits you'll take next term, and the planner works backward: (target × total credits after next term − quality points so far) ÷ credits next term. With a 2.8 over 45 credits and a 3.0 target, a 15-credit term needs (3.0 × 60 − 126) ÷ 15 = 3.6. If the answer is above the top of your scale, one term can't get you there, and the planner shows how many terms of straight A's it would take. The guide on how to raise your GPA covers what else moves the number.

Weighted vs. unweighted GPA

An unweighted GPA counts every class on the same 4.0 scale. A weighted GPA adds bonus points for harder classes, commonly +0.5 for Honors and +1.0 for AP or IB, so it can go above 4.0. Switch the calculator to High school to see both side by side. Which one colleges look at, and how to turn one into the other, is covered in our guide to weighted vs. unweighted GPA.

The grade scale this calculator uses

The calculator starts with the common 4.0 scale: A = 4.0, A− = 3.7, B+ = 3.3, B = 3.0, B− = 2.7, C+ = 2.3, C = 2.0, C− = 1.7, D+ = 1.3, D = 1.0, D− = 0.7 and F = 0. Your school may use other values, so edit the grade scale above if yours differs.

For the percentage range behind each letter, see the full chart in the 4.0 GPA scale guide, or convert a percentage to a letter grade.

GPA calculator without credits

Some report cards don't list credits. Turn on "All classes count equally" and every class gets the same weight. Your GPA is then the plain average of your grade points: an A, a B and a C give (4 + 3 + 2) ÷ 3 = 3.00.

If your classes do carry different credits, leave the option off. A plain average gives a 1-credit elective the same pull as a 4-credit core class.

What counts and what doesn't

An F counts. It adds zero quality points, but its credits stay in the total. An A and an F at 3 credits each come to 12 ÷ 6 = 2.00.

P and W usually don't count. On most transcripts they add no grade points and no GPA credits. If your school records a failing withdrawal as WF, check how it's treated before you rely on this number.

Why your result might not match your transcript

A small gap usually has a simple cause. Check these before you assume either number is wrong:

  • Your school uses a different scale, such as 3.67 for an A− instead of 3.7, or gives an A+ more than 4.0. Edit the grade scale above to match.
  • A retaken class replaced the earlier grade on your transcript, but both attempts are in your list here. Remove the attempt your school drops, or use the college GPA calculator, which handles retakes.
  • Transfer credits count toward your degree but often not toward your GPA at the new school.
  • Your high school rounds each semester grade, or adds a bonus for Honors and AP classes that's different from the example values here.

Your registrar's number is the official one. Use this calculator to plan, and the transcript to report.

How to find your official GPA

  • In college, the unofficial transcript or degree audit in your student portal usually lists your GPA, your GPA credits (sometimes called GPA hours) and your quality points. Enter those numbers in the cumulative GPA calculator for an exact match.
  • In high school, your report card or transcript shows the GPA your school calculates. Your counselor can tell you whether it's weighted and which classes get a bonus.
  • For applications, some systems work out their own number: the University of California uses a UC GPA, and law schools see an LSAC GPA.

If this calculator and your transcript disagree, the transcript is the official record.

What is a good GPA?

It depends on what the GPA is for. The average U.S. high school graduate finished with a 3.11 in 2019, according to the federal High School Transcript Study. In college, federal financial aid rules require students in programs longer than two years to have at least a C average, or its equivalent, by the end of their second academic year (34 CFR 668.34). Many scholarships and selective programs ask for much more. Our guide to what counts as a good GPA puts 2.5, 3.0, 3.5 and 4.0 in context. Frequently asked questions

What is a GPA?

A grade point average turns your letter grades into one number, usually on a 4.0 scale. Each class is weighted by its credits, so a class that meets more often or carries more credit counts more.

Does an F count in my GPA?

Yes. An F adds zero quality points, but its credits stay in the total, so it pulls the GPA down. An A and an F in two 3-credit classes give 12 ÷ 6 = 2.00.

Do pass/fail classes affect my GPA?

Usually not. A passing grade in a pass/fail class adds credits to your transcript but no grade points. Some schools count a failed pass/fail class as an F, so check your school's policy.

What's my GPA with all A's and one B?

If every class carries the same credits, four A's and one B give (4 × 4.0 + 3.0) ÷ 5 = 3.80. If the B is in a class with more credits than the others, it pulls the GPA down further, so enter your real credits above.

What grades do I need for a 3.5 GPA?

A 3.5 sits halfway between a B (3.0) and an A (4.0). With equal credits, half A's and half B's give exactly 3.5, and so do an A− and a B+ (3.7 and 3.3). To raise a cumulative GPA to 3.5, use the target planner above.

Can I check my GPA before final grades are in?

Yes. Enter the grades you expect and the calculator gives you an estimate. To see what you need on a final exam to keep a grade, use the final grade calculator.

Does this work for quarters or trimesters?

It does. Add one term for each quarter or trimester. The math is the same: quality points divided by credits, whatever the length of the term.
